No point of no return survives both definitions

The question was whether a bloom reaches a state where changing the drivers can no longer prevent it. Two independent tests were run against the same 617 pre-onset observations, and they do not agree anywhere.

The model's answer depends almost entirely on what you allow to change

Each rung adds levers. Chlorophyll-derived features are excluded throughout: they are the bloom itself, so moving them relabels the outcome rather than intervening on it.

The model-free test finds nothing at all

For every pre-onset observation, its 50 nearest historical analogues — drawn from other station-years and matched to within 30 days of the same day of year — were checked for how often they went on to bloom. If a state ever made a bloom inevitable, some analogue set would approach 100 %. None comes close.

A typical bloom onset has one water-quality reading in the month before it

Across clean onsets — episodes where the station's previous reading sat at or below the 10 µg/L threshold, so the onset is a genuine below-to-above transition — the pre-onset record is sparse enough to change what questions the data can answer.

This is why every in-situ series in the atlas is drawn as unconnected points. Joining three dots spread over four months would render a curve that was never measured, and the eye would read the interpolation as evidence. The densely-sampled layers — wind and gust, river discharge, astronomy, and the roughly 3.5-day satellite Kd(490) — are the only signals that genuinely resolve a run-up, and those are drawn as lines.

The consequence for the work: individual events are illustration, not evidence. Any claim about what precedes a bloom has to come from pooling many events, where the sparse per-event samples add up to a usable distribution.

Two bloom regimes, not one

Onsets are bimodal. Summer leads on raw episode count, but sampling effort is heavily concentrated in July and August; normalized by effort, the late-winter mode is the higher-rate one. Both modes are real, and they are driven by different things — a cold-water diatom bloom at 0–5 °C has little in common with a late-summer one. Pooling them would let opposite temperature and photoperiod signals cancel.

The WHO alert levels do not apply to this water body

They are worth stating precisely, because they are the numbers people reach for first. The World Health Organization's Guidelines on Recreational Water Quality (Volume 1, 2021) defines an Alert Level Framework:

Two independent mismatches

WHO scopes this framework to freshwater and brackish cyanobacteria, and publishes no chlorophyll-a alert values for marine harmful algal blooms — its position is that marine HABs act on health mainly through contaminated seafood. Long Island Sound is a marine estuary, and its highest-rate blooms are cold-water diatoms at 0–5 °C. Wrong water body, wrong taxa. These lines are context for orientation, never a validation target.

The project's own threshold is stricter than every published comparator — half the NJDEP and Florida adverse-effects line, a quarter of Florida's bloom-present line. That is a defensible early-warning choice, but it is the direct cause of the roughly 19 % positive rate in the record, and it means “bloom” here denotes an early biomass exceedance rather than a visible bloom. Any comparison against another program's skill numbers has to carry that difference explicitly.
